How it works

Four steps and you're done

The one goal behind all of this: nobody should spend more than 45 minutes preparing a 30-minute ritual. If you finish the conversation feeling you still have a deck to build, it failed.

  1. 01

    Paste the prompt into Claude Code, it sets itself up

    Take the one for your ritual from the list below and drop it into Claude Code, the desktop app or the terminal, not the browser chat: this first step needs it to run a command. It checks whether it already has what it needs and installs it if not, a few extra seconds the first time only. Your ritual is already in the prompt, so it gets straight to work.

  2. 02

    It asks what it needs, in one block

    Never one question per message, which is the main reason people give up halfway. Answer in shorthand, three words a line is plenty, or drag in notes, a brief, a screenshot. Missing a fact you'd rather not guess? Ask it to look it up: it'll say what it searched and where the answer came from.

  3. 03

    You read the plan before it becomes slides

    A text version of the whole deck, section by section, easier to correct than a layout. Say what's off, what needs more depth, or send more material, and it folds that in. Nothing gets locked into a slide until you've seen it written out plainly.

  4. 04

    You get the branded deck, right where you're chatting

    The real template, filled, with speaker notes that say what to cover out loud and the timing for each slide. It lands in your working folder, no download button to hunt for. Want it in Google Slides instead of PowerPoint? File → Import slides does that in one click, on your side.

The 8 rituals

Find yours

Still undecided? Start from the material you already have on hand: a mission you just left, a tool you just tried, an object you can't stop thinking about, a question you keep arguing about with a colleague.

Remote, 30 min · 7 slides · 18 min talking, 10 min questions

T'REX 🦖

A slice of a mission, or the whole mission: the context, the problem you hit, the approach you took, and what it changed for the client and for you.

The trap

Telling the mission in chronological order. Six months never fit into 18 minutes, and the good part ends up squeezed onto the last slide.

What Claude will ask you

  1. The client in one line, and how design-mature they were
  2. What you were asked for, what was really going on, and how you found out
  3. Your 2 or 3 moves, and the artefact you can show for each one
  4. What changed: a decision, a habit, a metric if you have one
  5. What you'd do differently, concrete enough for a colleague to apply on Monday

Remote, 30 min · 6 slides · 12 min of live demo, the rest around it

T'LAB 🔬

AI tooling, craft, a tool you found recently. Hands-on enough that the room can go and redo it themselves.

The trap

Replaying the vendor demo. Everyone can watch the marketing video on their own time. The value sits in running the tool on something you actually had to deliver.

What Claude will ask you

  1. Which real task you pointed it at, and the before / after in minutes
  2. Price, access, and what the tool does with your data (saying what you don't know counts as an answer)
  3. The three steps of your demo
  4. Two places where it falls apart, bluntly
  5. The prompt or config you actually used

Remote, 30 min · 7 slides · Two decisions decoded, not four name-dropped

That's a Banger 🥇

A short exposé on design that has nothing to do with our screens, so we can pinch ideas from other domains and from people working under very different constraints.

The trap

The Wikipedia summary. Dates and a timeline are not a banger. What works: two deliberate decisions, the option that got rejected, and the constraint that forced the call.

What Claude will ask you

  1. The object, and your personal hook: why this one
  2. Who made it, when, and under what constraint
  3. Two deliberate decisions, with the rejected option each time
  4. The detail nobody notices, the one you'd zoom into
  5. What we can pinch, translated into something concrete on a product

Remote, 30 min · 6 slides · The most relaxed slot of the month

Passion Fruit 🍍

You talk about something you love, what it quietly taught you as a designer, and the tribe gets to know you a bit better along the way. No need to be an expert.

The trap

Forcing the parallel. If the passion is bread and the conclusion is that it taught you agile, everybody can tell. Two honest bridges beat three invented ones.

What Claude will ask you

  1. The passion in three words, and how you got into it
  2. The mechanism outsiders never get to see
  3. Two or three things it taught you, anchored in something you did last month
  4. What you can show: photos, a short video, an object held up to camera
  5. How somebody would start this weekend

Remote, 30 min · 7 slides · Two consultants, a single question

Back 2 Back 🕺💃

Two people answer the same question about their missions, side by side, to see how much the context changes the answer.

The trap

Smoothing both answers into one tidy narrative. The friction is the subject of the session. Where you disagree, that goes on the slide.

What Claude will ask you

  1. The question you're both answering
  2. Your context lines: client, sector, scope, design maturity, team, how long you've been there, tooling
  3. Your answer in one sentence, filled in separately from your partner's
  4. Where it's stuck and what's working, two of each
  5. Reading the other answer: the one thing you'd pinch

Remote, 30 min · 5 slides · 12 min of case, 18 min of debate

Change My Mind 💬

You come with a statement you actually believe, you make the case, and you spend the rest of the slot being argued with. Responsible AI, accessibility, gender equality in product design, where the line sits between PM and designer.

The trap

Picking a thesis nobody will contest. "Accessibility matters" gets nods and silence. A debate needs a claim with a cost attached, one you'd still defend in a room where half the people work differently.

What Claude will ask you

  1. The statement, in one sentence, as a claim rather than a topic
  2. Why it's worth arguing about now
  3. Your two arguments, and what you saw that made you believe each one
  4. The strongest argument against you, stated the way its defenders would state it
  5. Who in the tribe you expect to disagree, and what they'll say

Turning a subject into a claim is the part Claude works hardest on: it comes back with two or three sharpened phrasings of your thesis and you pick. On subjects that touch identity, it steers the claim toward a practice rather than toward people, and says why.

In person, 60 min · Title + 4 slides · 20 min of guest, 30 min of questions

Ask Me Anything 🎤

Someone from outside Thiga comes to talk: a sector we never see (games, furniture, fashion, a startup) or a craft we don't have in house.

The trap

Letting it drift into a fifty-minute talk with five rushed minutes at the end. The name of the ritual is a clue.

What Claude will ask you

  1. Who the guest is, what they've built, and why them now
  2. What came out of your prep call, including what's off-limits and whether we can record
  3. The surprising thing you learned that isn't on their LinkedIn
  4. Which themes the tribe wants to hear them on
  5. Who keeps time, and it shouldn't be you

On top of the deck, Claude hands you the bank of 12 questions split across four buckets, and the question-collection message to post in the tribe's Slack a week ahead.

In person, 60 min · 8 slides · Co-animated with a thematic Tribe

Let's Work(shop) 🧰

A workshop on something this Tribe, or Thiga, genuinely needs moved forward. One exercise, one output.

The trap

Cramming in three exercises. And the quieter trap: an output nobody owns. A pleasant hour that evaporates, and that is exactly what makes people stop signing up.

What Claude will ask you

  1. What precise output people walk out holding, in one sentence
  2. Which thematic Tribe, and which objective it serves
  3. The question the room works on
  4. Who owns the output afterwards, and where it lands
  5. How many tables you expect, and the real time slot

Claude runs the interview backwards: the output first, then who owns it, then the exercise, then the framing, and only then the slides.

The guardrails

What Claude won't do

A mission feedback deck travels to colleagues who work at other clients. These four rules live inside the skill, they are not negotiated case by case.

  • No invented numbers

    No metric, client name, quote or result that you didn't give. When a figure is missing, the slide shows [your number here] and Claude says so out loud. A decorative percentage in a mission feedback is a client-trust incident, not a typo.

  • Client anonymised by default

    "A French insurance group, 12,000 people" does the job. User data and names blurred in screenshots, relative metrics rather than absolute ones, and never a link to a client Figma or Miro: a screenshot instead.

  • Write in French or English, the deck comes out in English

    Claude follows whatever language you write in, and it reads a French brief as happily as an English one. Paste your notes as they are, no need to translate anything first. The deck itself is always in English, like every deck in the tribe.

  • The slide ceiling is a ceiling

    Seven slides for a T'REX means seven, not eight. More slides is not more generous, it's a longer meeting. The READ ME slide and the TIP boxes are stripped from the deliverable.

  • A searched fact always carries its source

    Claude only looks things up when it helps and never for rituals about your own experience. Anything it finds online is labelled as such in the prep notes, right next to what you told it yourself, and the two never get blurred into one.
