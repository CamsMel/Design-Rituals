# Shared conventions

Read this before every deck. It holds what the seven rituals have in common:
how a slide is written, what may never leave the building, and the gates that
apply whatever the format.

## Slide grammar

**One idea per slide, 25 words on screen maximum.** Not a style preference — a
remote audience reads the slide or listens to the speaker, never both. Words on
the slide compete with the person talking.

If a paragraph appears, it belongs in the speaker notes. That is the test: could
this be said instead of shown? Then say it.

Write in **English**, the whole deck, always. The conversation follows the
consultant, usually French; the deliverable does not.

Fragments beat sentences on a slide. "First designer they'd ever hired" lands
harder than "I was the first designer that the company had ever hired." Keep the
consultant's own words wherever they gave you any — a deck that sounds like the
speaker survives contact with the stage.

### Speaker notes

Every slide gets them. 2–3 sentences of what to actually say out loud, plus a
timing cue. Not a script to read — a reminder of the point of the slide and
where the story goes next.

Good: *"Tell the moment you found out — the interview nobody had run. Go easy on
the client, that gap is normal and it's usually why they hired a designer. 3 min"*

Bad: *"Explain the discovery phase and the methodology used."*

Timings are in the templates already; keep them. They add up to the format, and
a consultant who overruns eats the questions, which is the part the tribe came
for.

### The bracket rule

`[your number here]`, `[client name]`, `[the metric]` — leave brackets in the
deck whenever the honest answer is "we don't know yet", and list them in the
handover message. A bracket is a visible, fillable gap. An invented figure is
invisible and wrong.

## Confidentiality

Printed at the foot of the T'REX, T'LAB and Back 2 Back templates. It applies to
all seven:

- Client anonymised unless the mission is public. "A French insurance group,
  12 000 people" does the job and nobody has to check anything.
- User data, names and figures blurred in screenshots. Do a redaction pass
  before pasting, not after.
- Relative metrics rather than absolute ones. "Cut the tag list by 85%" travels;
  "225 tags down to 30" is a client's internal number.
- Never a link to a client Figma or Miro. Screenshot it.

Applies to the deck and to anything posted alongside it in the tribe's Slack.

## Never invent

No metric, client name, quote, or result that the consultant did not give you.
This is the rule with the sharpest edge, because a plausible number is easy to
produce and impossible to spot later.

A decorative percentage in a mission feedback is a client-trust incident, not a
typo. If a figure is missing, write `[your number here]` and say so in the
handover.

When a consultant gives you something ambiguous — "I think it was about a third
faster" — keep their hedge on the slide. "Roughly a third faster" is honest and
still useful. Hardening it to "33% faster" is the failure mode.

## The interview

**One block, five questions maximum, never one per message.** A consultant three
days from their slot will not survive eight round trips. If you need more than
five, the angle isn't narrow enough yet — go back to step 2.

**Mine what they gave you first.** Notes, mission brief, meeting write-up,
transcript, an old deck: extract every answer you can and ask only about the
gaps. Say what you already took from it, so they can correct a bad reading:
*"From your brief I've got the sector, the team and the trigger. Three things
missing."*

**Accept telegraphic answers.** "insurance, 12k people, no research at all,
first designer" is a complete answer to the context question. Turning it into
slides is your job.

## Handover

Deliver the .pptx with a short list of what only a human can finish:

- visuals to drop into the dashed zones
- `[brackets]` still standing, named one by one
- screenshots to blur
- a read-through, because their name is on it

Keep it to a few lines. A long checklist undoes the feeling of being finished,
and that feeling was the deliverable.

## Quality gates for every ritual

Before handing over, check:

1. **Does the deck respect the slide ceiling?** The ceiling is a ceiling. Cut,
   don't negotiate.
2. **Is there a single invented fact?** Scan every number, name and quote back
   to something the consultant actually said.
3. **Is the READ ME FIRST slide gone, and every TIP box with it?** They exist to
   help someone fill the template, not to be presented.
4. **Does the last slide end on an open question to the tribe?** Never a "Thank
   you" or "Any questions?" slide — every template already closes on a real
   question. If the question is weak, that is the one to rewrite.
5. **Does any slide carry a paragraph?** Move it to the notes.
6. **Would the consultant recognise their own voice reading it out loud?** If it
   reads like a consultancy brochure, run it through `tone-of-voice` then
   `anti-ai-writing`.

## Handing off to other skills

- `pptx` — anything the fill script doesn't cover: inserting an image, splitting

- `thiga-visual-generator` — a branded visual to drop into a slide.
- `tone-of-voice` then `anti-ai-writing` — any prose longer than a bullet.
