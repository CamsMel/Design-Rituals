# Change My Mind 💬 — the debate

**The promise.** Someone comes with a statement they actually believe, makes the
case in twelve minutes, and spends the next eighteen being argued with. The
subjects are the ones the tribe genuinely disagrees on: responsible AI,
accessibility, gender equality in product design, where the line sits between PM
and designer.

**Format.** Remote, 30 min. 12 min of case, 18 min of debate. 5 slides.
Template: `assets/templates/change-my-mind.pptx` · `--template change-my-mind`

**The trap.** Picking a thesis nobody will contest. "Accessibility matters" gets
nods and silence, and a silent room is a wasted slot. A debate needs a claim
with a cost attached, something the speaker would still defend in a room where
half the people work differently.

The quieter trap: defending the position to the death. The ritual is called
Change My Mind, not Prove Me Right. If nothing would move the speaker, this is
the wrong format for them and it's worth saying so early rather than at the end.

## Sharpening the thesis is the whole job

For every other ritual, framing means choosing which slice of the material to
tell. Here it means turning a topic into a claim, and it's where most of your
value sits. Consultants arrive with a subject ("I want to talk about responsible
AI") and a subject cannot be debated.

Test any candidate thesis against three questions:

1. **Could a reasonable colleague refuse it?** If not, it's a mood, not a thesis.
   "Collaboration is important" fails. "PMs should not write the problem
   statement alone" passes.
2. **Does it cost the speaker something?** A thesis with no trade-off attached
   invites agreement. Name what they're giving up by holding it.
3. **Is it about practice rather than people?** This matters most on the
   subjects that touch identity. "Our hiring loop filters out women" puts the
   room on trial. "Take-home exercises cost us the candidates we say we want"
   argues about a practice and gets a real debate. Steer the thesis toward the
   practice, and say why you're doing it.

Offer two or three candidate phrasings of their thesis and let them pick. Seeing
their own belief sharpened is faster than being asked to sharpen it themselves.

## Slide plan

**1. Cover — `CHANGE MY MIND 💬`** · 1 min
`[DATE]` · `[Your thesis, one sentence, stated flat, not as a question]` ·
`[Your name]` · `[Client or anonymised descriptor]`
The thesis goes in quotes on the cover, and it is the whole slide. Speaker notes
suggest the meme staging: the statement on a sheet of paper, held up to camera.
It reads as a joke and it does real work, because it commits the speaker in
front of everyone.

**2. `MY THESIS, AND WHY NOW`** · 2 min
`[The statement again, in your own words]` ·
`[Why it is worth arguing about now, in one line]`
"Why now" is what stops the session being a philosophy seminar: a client
situation, a tool that changed, something they read that annoyed them.

**3. `THE CASE FOR IT`** · 5 min — two columns
WHAT I ARGUE: `[Argument one, one line]` · `[Argument two, one line]`
WHERE IT COMES FROM: `[The mission, the project or the reading behind it]` ·
`[What you saw that made you believe it]`
Two arguments, not five. Every extra argument is one the room has to queue
behind. The right-hand column is what makes it a debate rather than an opinion:
an argument with no experience behind it gets dismissed in thirty seconds.

**4. `WHERE I MIGHT BE WRONG`** · 3 min — a 5×2 table
`[the best argument against you, stated fairly]` (The strongest objection) ·
`[the strawman you want off the table]` (What I'm not arguing) ·
`[the part of your case you would drop first]` (Where I'm least sure) ·
`[the evidence or experience that would move you]` (What would change my mind)
The slide that makes the ritual work. Naming the best counter-argument yourself
buys a real debate instead of eighteen minutes of people explaining something
the speaker already knew.

**5. `THE FLOOR IS YOURS`** · 18 min
`[Opening question: the one that splits the room]` ·
`[Second question, for when the first is exhausted]` ·
`[A question aimed at whoever disagrees most]`
Plus the ground rule in the highlighted band: challenge the claim, never the
person. This slide stays up for the whole debate, which is why the rule lives on
it rather than in the notes. The deck ends here on purpose: you don't summarise
a debate you just opened, and the closing "here's what moved me" is spoken, not
slided.

## Interview

1. What's the statement, and do you actually believe it? One sentence, as a
   claim, not a topic.
2. Why is it worth arguing about now? A client situation, a tool that changed,
   something you read.
3. Your two arguments, and for each one what you saw that made you believe it.
4. The strongest argument against you, stated the way its defenders would state
   it. And what would genuinely change your mind.
5. Who in the tribe do you expect to disagree, and what will they say?

Question 5 does double duty: it surfaces the counter-arguments and it usually
produces the opening question for slide 5.

## Quality gates

- Could a reasonable colleague refuse the thesis? A claim everybody nods at
  produces silence, and silence is the failure mode of this format.
- Is the thesis about a practice rather than about people? On subjects that
  touch identity, this is the difference between a debate and an incident.
- Is the counter-argument on slide 4 stated fairly, or is it a strawman the
  speaker can knock down? The room can tell instantly.
- Is "what would change my mind" something that could actually happen? If
  nothing would move the speaker, say so out loud: they may want a different
  ritual.
- Are the three opening questions open, and does at least one of them invite
  disagreement directly? "Who here thinks this is wrong?" beats "any thoughts?"
- Is a moderator named who is not the speaker? Eighteen minutes of remote debate
  needs someone watching the queue, and the speaker cannot argue and facilitate
  at the same time.
